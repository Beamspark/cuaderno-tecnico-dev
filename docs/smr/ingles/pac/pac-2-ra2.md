### Texto de Referencia: *Net Neutrality is Just the Start of an Open Internet*

> US regulators are deciding whether to keep the internet open. This decision could affect internet rules worldwide.
>
> Net neutrality means your internet provider can’t make one service easier to use than another. For example, it can’t make the Guardian faster to access than the Telegraph just because one paid more money.
>
> The current fight over net neutrality is mainly about US cable and content companies. On February 26, the FCC, which regulates US telecommunications, will vote on a plan called "Preserving the Open Internet." People say important principles are at stake, but they don't all agree on what those principles are.
>
> The internet isn’t unlimited. Managing internet traffic is necessary. When you make a phone call, your voice is sampled many times a second and reassembled quickly. When you use the internet, the data is sent as fast as the network allows, unless something slows it down. If voice calls and internet data interfere with each other, voice calls usually adapt, but data-heavy services like video might not work well.
>
> First, net neutrality means ensuring internet providers don’t block or slow down access to legal content or services. Most people agree on this idea. However, some worry that enforcing this could stop some kinds of internet traffic, like peer-to-peer file sharing.
>
> Second, net neutrality involves managing internet traffic details, like "fast lanes" where ISPs get paid to prioritize some content. This is more controversial. Companies like Netflix already pay for better service because they use a lot of internet traffic.
>
> The FCC’s proposal also looks at mobile networks and how these deals should be managed. The US is starting to agree that regulating bandwidth to protect public interest is important, similar to other countries.
>
> Net neutrality is a big issue for internet activists in the US, who see cable companies as the bad guys. But it's also about the monopolistic US telecom market and resistance to internet regulation. Big internet companies like Facebook, Google, and Amazon benefit from net neutrality debates.
>
> Net neutrality won’t solve all problems. Issues like unequal internet speeds for different sites and powerful content delivery networks remain. To have a truly open internet, we need to address all these factors. Net neutrality is a good start, but not the final solution.
